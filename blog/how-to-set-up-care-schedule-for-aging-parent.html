How to Set Up a Care Schedule for an Aging Parent — Free Weekly Template Included

A calm, practical system for organising daily care, sharing responsibility and protecting your own wellbeing as a caregiver.

✍️ Deep Darji 📅 Published Oct 24, 2026 🔄 Updated Oct 24, 2026 ⏱ 4 min read 🏷 Caregiving • Elder Care • Family

Knowing how to set up a care schedule for an aging parent turns a stressful, scattered situation into a clear routine everyone can follow. A good schedule covers daily needs, medicines, appointments, meals, safety checks and your own rest.

A caregiver often starts with "just helping out" and ends up carrying everything. This guide shows how to share the load and keep your parent safe, comfortable and independent for as long as possible.

Quick answer: List your parent's needs, write down medicines and contacts, map the week by time of day, assign tasks to family members, add a backup plan and respite time, then review the schedule every month.

Step 1: Assess What Your Parent Needs

Start with activities of daily living (bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, moving around) and everyday tasks such as cooking, shopping, managing money and taking medicines. Note which tasks your parent does alone, with reminders, or with hands-on help. Ask their doctor for a clear picture of health needs.

Step 2: Gather the Key Information

  • Full medicine list with doses and timing (ask the doctor or pharmacist to confirm).
  • Doctor, hospital and pharmacy contacts.
  • Allergies, conditions and insurance details.
  • Emergency contacts and a copy of important documents.

Keep this on one printed page on the fridge and in a shared phone note.

Add weekly items such as doctor visits, grocery shopping, laundry and bill payments. A gentle daily movement routine helps too. See our low-impact home workout for seniors.

Step 4: Share Responsibilities Fairly

Hold a short family meeting (video call is fine). Let each person pick tasks that suit their time, skills and budget. Remote relatives can handle phone calls, bills, online orders or paying for help. Write the plan down so nobody has to guess.

Step 5: Use Simple Tools

  • A shared calendar for appointments and shifts.
  • Phone alarms and a weekly pill organiser for medicines.
  • A group chat for daily updates.
  • A notebook by the bed for observations to report to the doctor.

Step 6: Plan for Backups and Respite

Decide in advance who steps in when someone is sick or travelling. Respite care (short-term relief from a trained helper or care centre) is not a luxury. It prevents burnout. Keep a list of local agencies and emergency numbers.

Step 7: Make the Home Safer

  • Remove loose rugs and clear walkways.
  • Add grab bars and non-slip mats in the bathroom.
  • Improve lighting, especially at night.
  • Keep medicines locked or organised and out of confusion's way.

Step 8: Review Every Month

Needs change. Ask your parent how they feel, check what is and is not working, and update the schedule. Involve your parent in decisions wherever possible. Dignity and choice matter.

Medical note: This guide is general information. Consult your parent's doctor or a qualified care professional for medical decisions, medicines and dementia care.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I make a care schedule for my elderly parent?

List their needs, medicines and appointments, then map tasks by time of day, assign each task to a person, and review monthly.

How do siblings share caregiving fairly?

Hold a family meeting, divide tasks by time, skills and budget, and write down who does what. Remote siblings can help with bills, calls and paid support.

What is respite care?

Respite care is short-term support that gives the main caregiver a break, delivered at home or in a care facility.

How often should a care plan be updated?

At least monthly, and any time health or mobility changes.
